## How to read this file

Each guideline has up to three parts:

- **Standard**: a paraphrase of what ISO 24495-1 says. Clauses 1 to the first part of 5.2.3 are summarized from the publicly available preview of the standard. For later clauses only the official guideline title is confirmed, so the Standard line gives the title and, at most, a related statement from a confirmed clause, naming that clause.
- **Apply**: practical guidance written for this skill, consistent with the standard's definition and principles. It is not ISO text.
- **Check**: questions to ask when you evaluate a draft or review someone else's text.

Statements credited to the International Plain Language Federation (IPLF), which initiated the standard, come from its commentary on the standard, not from the standard's text.

When you cite the standard to a user, cite the clause number and title, and present Apply and Check content as your own advice.

## Core definitions

- **Plain language**: communication in which wording, structure, and design are so clear that intended readers can easily find what they need, understand what they find, and use that information. (Adopted from the International Plain Language Federation's definition.)
- **Reader**: a member of the intended audience. This includes people who view, hear, or touch a document, people who skim or scan it for particular information, and people who have it read to them by a person or a device, not only people who read it from start to finish. A document can have several audiences; when their needs conflict, the primary audience's needs come first.
- **Document**: printed or digital information that is mainly text, such as an email, error message, contract, form, podcast script, or web page.
- **Information design**: combining text, typography, images, and multimedia visually so that readers can find, understand, and use information. It makes structure and content visible.
- **Evaluation**: assessing how well readers find, understand, and use information.

The standard's introduction frames plain language as putting readers first: what they want and need to know, their interest, expertise, and literacy, and the context in which they will use the document. It focuses on how successfully readers can use a document rather than on readability formulas.

## Principle 1: Relevant

Readers get what they need. This principle covers what to settle before drafting.

### 5.1.2 Identify the readers

- **Standard**: Identify who the readers are and consider their literacy and language skills, cultural backgrounds, subject knowledge, accessibility needs and preferences, and the languages they understand. Provide the document in a language they know, which may mean meeting official-language requirements or translating. Ways to learn about readers include past communication with them, interviews, surveys, and existing research.
- **Apply**: Pin down the primary reader in one sentence (for example, "tenants of building B, mostly older residents, who have never applied for this subsidy"). Note what they already know and what they will not know.
- **Check**: Would a word, assumption, or reference in the text be unfamiliar to this reader?

### 5.1.3 Identify the readers' purpose

- **Standard**: Identify why readers will read the document. They may be following instructions, deciding whether to do something, trying to understand a topic, finding out what the author wants them to do, learning about an interest, or gaining knowledge for a specific goal such as a licence.
- **Apply**: Write the reader's purpose as a task: "decide whether to renew," "complete the form correctly," "know what changes on 1 March." The purpose decides what goes first.
- **Check**: After reading, can the reader do the task the text exists for?

### 5.1.4 Identify the context in which readers will read the document

- **Standard**: Consider where readers will read it, what technology they will use, how much time and attention they will have, how interested they will be, and their emotional state.
- **Apply**: A worried patient reading on a phone needs the reassurance and the next step at the top, in a few lines. A professional comparing options at a desk can use a table. Bad news or a demand for payment needs extra care with tone.
- **Check**: Does the length, layout, and tone suit the moment the reader will actually be in?

### 5.1.5 Select the document type or types

- **Standard**: Choose the document type or types that fit the readers' characteristics, purpose, and context. An alternative to a document, such as a video, may serve readers better.
- **Apply**: In a rewrite, keep the user's document type and channel unless they ask to change it. In a write task, pick the type that fits (a short message, a letter, a step-by-step guide, an FAQ) and confirm it in Step 1 when the choice is unclear.
- **Check**: Is this the form in which readers can most easily use the information?

### 5.1.6 Select content that readers need

- **Standard**: Keep readers' needs first even when the author's needs require some content. Identify the questions readers need answered; select content that fits their characteristics, purpose, and context and the document type; leave out what they do not need (required supplementary information goes separately, see 5.2.5); consider the author's purposes too. Select content ethically: accurate, not false or misleading, and without hiding or omitting what readers need to know.
- **Apply**: Make a list of reader questions and answer each one the reader needs. Cut organizational history, self-praise, and internal process unless the reader needs them. Never cut a condition, cost, deadline, risk, exception, or right that affects the reader. Resolve essential missing facts through Step 1; omit unknown details only when the remaining text still serves the reader's purpose accurately.
- **Check**: Is anything the reader needs missing or hard to see? Is anything present that this reader does not need? Could any sentence mislead?

## Principle 2: Findable

Readers can easily find what they need. They should be able to tell quickly what the document is about and whether it serves their purpose.

### 5.2.2 Structure the document for readers

- **Standard**: Group related information and order it in a way that makes sense to readers: put the most important message where readers can easily find it, commonly at the beginning; build new information on what readers already know; present instructions and processes in time order; put information most readers need before information only some need; where not following a process can cause damage or harm, put warnings about what not to do before the instructions about what to do.
- **Apply**: Open with the main message or required action in one or two sentences. Then follow the order in which the reader will think or act. Put exceptions and special cases after the general rule.
- **Check**: Could a reader who reads only the first few lines act correctly? Does any warning appear after the step it is meant to prevent?

### 5.2.3 Use information design techniques that enable readers to find information

- **Standard**: Use inclusive information design. Show relations between elements visually: make important elements prominent, place elements that belong together close to each other, and make elements with the same function look alike. (The preview ends partway through this clause; further techniques are not summarized here.)
- **Apply**: Use what the destination supports: numbered lists for steps, bullet lists for parallel items, tables for values and comparisons, emphasis on the one or two items that must not be missed, and space between groups. Keep formatting consistent so readers learn the pattern. Do not emphasize so much that nothing stands out.
- **Check**: Can the reader find a specific item (a date, a fee, a phone number) in a few seconds?

### 5.2.4 Use headings to help readers predict what comes next

- **Standard**: (Title confirmed.) The Principle 2 overview (5.2.1) notes that headings are one of many techniques that help readers predict what comes next, especially in documents longer than a few paragraphs.
- **Apply**: Write headings from the reader's side: questions they ask ("How do I apply?") or statements of the point ("Apply by 30 June"). Avoid vague labels ("General", "Other information"). A short text may need no headings.
- **Check**: Reading only the headings, does the reader get the outline and know where to look?

### 5.2.5 Keep supplementary information separate

- **Standard**: (Title confirmed.) Clause 5.1.6 refers here for required supplementary information.
- **Apply**: Put background, legal references, detailed eligibility rules, and rare cases after the main content, in a labeled section, an appendix, or a link, so they remain available without blocking the main message.
- **Check**: Does required but secondary content interrupt the path to the main action?

## Principle 3: Understandable

Readers can easily understand what they find.

### 5.3.2 Choose familiar words

- **Standard**: (Title confirmed.)
- **Apply**: Choose familiar, precise words for these readers. Replace unfamiliar jargon, officialese, and internal names with clearer equivalents where meaning is preserved. Keep needed technical or legal terms consistent; explain them at first use when readers need that explanation. Expand or avoid unfamiliar abbreviations, while retaining familiar ones when they are clearer. Apply the exact-string boundary in Step 4 when referring to existing labels, commands, or other text readers must match or enter.
- **Check**: Would the primary reader stop at a word or have to look it up? Does an explanation help these readers, or repeat what they already know? Can they still match any referenced wording to the actual interface or artifact?

### 5.3.3 Write clear sentences

- **Standard**: (Title confirmed.)
- **Apply**: Make the actor and the action clear: who does what. Put the subject and the main verb close together, and let the main point appear early. Prefer verbs over abstract nouns. Address the reader directly when it fits. State things positively when a negative adds nothing, and avoid double negatives. Keep a condition next to the action it limits.
- **Check**: Could the sentence be read two ways? Is it clear who must act?

### 5.3.4 Write concise sentences

- **Standard**: (Title confirmed.)
- **Apply**: One main idea per sentence is a good default. Remove filler, redundant pairs, and throat-clearing openers. Split long chains of conditions into a list. Concise means no wasted words, not a word count; some readers need an explanation that takes more words.
- **Check**: Can any words go without loss of meaning? Does any sentence carry more than one main idea?

### 5.3.5 Write clear and concise paragraphs

- **Standard**: (Title confirmed.)
- **Apply**: Give each paragraph one topic and state it in the first sentence. Keep paragraphs short enough to scan on the destination screen or page.
- **Check**: Could the reader summarize each paragraph from its first sentence?

### 5.3.6 Consider including images and multimedia

- **Standard**: (Title confirmed.) The standard's definition of "image" (3.6) covers visual representations such as charts, diagrams, flowcharts, icons, maps, photographs, and tables.
- **Apply**: In text output, use tables and worked examples (for example, a calculated fee for a typical case) where they explain better than prose. When the requested deliverable includes an image or diagram, include the finished visual in a format the destination supports. In review mode, suggest a visual when it would solve a specific reader problem. Any visual should repeat or clarify the text, not carry essential information alone.
- **Check**: Is there a comparison, process, or calculation that a table or example would make easier?

### 5.3.7 Project a respectful tone

- **Standard**: (Title confirmed.)
- **Apply**: Write as one capable adult to another. Avoid blaming ("you failed to"), threats, sarcasm, and condescension. Explain the reason for a requirement. Use inclusive, non-stereotyping language. Choose the formality level the readers expect in that language and situation.
- **Check**: Would a reader in a stressful situation feel respected and helped, not scolded?

### 5.3.8 Ensure that the document is cohesive

- **Standard**: (Title confirmed.)
- **Apply**: Use one term for one thing throughout. Make every reference ("this", "the above", "해당") point to something obvious. Use transitions that show how parts relate (because, so, after that, except). Keep headings, lists, and formatting consistent so the whole reads as one document.
- **Check**: Does each part connect to the one before it? Does any term change mid-document?

## Principle 4: Usable

Readers can easily use the information. The standard states that following the guidelines under Principles 1 to 3 makes it likely that readers can use a document, but the only way to make sure is to keep evaluating it with the guidelines under Principle 4. It also states that the four principles are interdependent, so they are not a sequential process and are applied together.

### 5.4.2 Evaluate the document continually as it is developed

- **Standard**: (Title confirmed.)
- **Apply**: This is the evaluation you can do yourself. Reread the draft as the primary reader in their context and walk through each reader question. In a rewrite, check the full source for reader-needed or author-required information that was lost or changed. Confirm that removed material was irrelevant or redundant and that required exact strings still match. Revise and check again.
- **Check**: Did you check the draft against reader tasks, not just against style rules?

### 5.4.3 Evaluate the document further with readers

- **Standard**: (Title confirmed.)
- **Apply**: You cannot do this yourself. If the user asks about testing, suggest the methods in [Testing with real readers](#testing-with-real-readers).

### 5.4.4 Continue to evaluate readers' use of the document

- **Standard**: (Title confirmed.)
- **Apply**: If the user asks, suggest watching signals after publication: repeated questions, calls, complaints, form errors, or missed deadlines show where the text fails.

## Testing with real readers

The IPLF says the only way to know for sure that a document works is to test it with a sample of its readers. The methods below are common plain-language testing practices, not text from the standard. Suggest them when the user asks how to confirm a document works.

- **Task test**: Ask a few people from the primary audience to find a specific item or complete the task the document supports. Watch where they hesitate or go wrong.
- **Paraphrase test**: Ask readers to explain in their own words what the document asks them to do. Mismatches show where meaning is unclear.
- **Comparison**: Show two versions and ask which helps them act. Prefer performance evidence over stated preference.
- **Small and early**: A handful of readers from the right audience often reveals the main problems. Test drafts, not only final versions.

## What the standard does not do

- **Certification**: According to the IPLF, Part 1 is guidance and is not used for certification. Do not tell users that a text conforms to or is certified under ISO 24495-1.
- **Fixed metrics**: The standard focuses on how well readers can use a document rather than on mechanical measures such as readability formulas. The IPLF describes its word-level guidance as high level and language-neutral, and notes that countries may add language-specific practices, such as sentence length.
- **Easy language**: The standard distinguishes plain language (for a general audience) from easy language (for people with reading difficulties).
- **Scope**: It covers printed or digital information that is mainly text. It does not replace accessibility guidance such as WCAG or EN 301 549. Creators of podcasts and videos may still find it useful.
- **Other parts**: ISO 24495-2 covers legal communication and ISO 24495-3 covers science writing. This skill does not include their specific guidance.
